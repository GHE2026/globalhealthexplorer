EXPLORE

Why does Indigenous health matter?

Health is shaped by much more than healthcare. For Indigenous Peoples, understanding health requires attention to culture, identity, relationships, land, history, community, rights, social conditions and self-determination.

01

Health & Wellbeing

Wellbeing can include physical, mental, emotional, spiritual, cultural and social dimensions of life. Different Indigenous Peoples may understand and express wellbeing in different ways.

02

Equity

Health equity means understanding and addressing the conditions that create avoidable and unfair differences in health and access to care.

03

Indigenous Leadership

Indigenous communities are not simply recipients of health services. Indigenous Peoples are knowledge holders, leaders, researchers, educators, practitioners and decision-makers.

THINK DIFFERENTLY

Indigenous Peoples are diverse.

There is no single Indigenous culture, worldview, health system or experience. Indigenous Peoples live in many different regions and communities and have distinct languages, histories, traditions, governance systems and contemporary realities.

Many Peoples Indigenous identities and nations are diverse.
Many Histories Historical experiences differ across regions and nations.
Many Knowledge Systems Indigenous knowledge is diverse, living and evolving.
Many Strengths Culture, relationships and community can be important sources of strength and resilience.
LEARN

What does Indigenous health mean?

Indigenous health refers to the health and wellbeing of Indigenous Peoples, including the physical, mental, emotional, spiritual, cultural and social dimensions of wellbeing.

It also includes the systems, relationships and conditions that influence health: family and community connections, access to land and resources, education, housing, income, safety, health services, language, culture and opportunities for self-determination.

Health is not simply the absence of disease.

A population-health perspective asks what enables people and communities to live healthy, meaningful and dignified lives.

A GHE QUESTION

What creates the conditions for wellbeing?

  • Culture and identity
  • Family and community
  • Connection to land and place
  • Education and opportunity
  • Safe housing and environments
  • Accessible and culturally safe care
  • Self-determination and participation
CULTURE & IDENTITY

Culture can be a source of health and strength.

Culture is not an optional addition to health. For many Indigenous Peoples, identity, language, family, community, land, ceremony, traditional knowledge and relationships are important dimensions of wellbeing.

01

Identity

A strong sense of identity and belonging can contribute to wellbeing and resilience.

02

Language

Indigenous languages can carry knowledge, history, identity and relationships across generations.

03

Community

Relationships with family, Elders, peers and community can provide belonging, knowledge and support.

04

Land & Place

Relationships with land and place may be deeply connected with identity, culture, livelihood and wellbeing.

05

Knowledge

Indigenous knowledge systems contain generations of observation, experience, practice and teaching.

06

Continuity

Passing knowledge and cultural practices between generations can strengthen identity and connection.

SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH

Health is shaped by the conditions in which people live.

Indigenous health inequities cannot be understood only by looking at individual behaviours or medical conditions.

01

Education

Access to safe, inclusive and culturally relevant education can influence lifelong opportunities.

02

Housing

Safe, adequate and affordable housing is an important foundation for health.

03

Income & Employment

Economic security influences choices, resources and opportunities for healthy living.

04

Environment

Environmental conditions, access to clean water and food, and the safety of local environments can affect health.

05

Healthcare Access

Availability, affordability, accessibility and cultural safety can influence whether people receive appropriate care.

06

Discrimination

Racism, discrimination and exclusion can affect health, wellbeing, trust and access to services.

HISTORY → PRESENT

Understanding history matters.

Indigenous health cannot be understood without considering the historical and contemporary forces that have shaped communities and health systems.

Across different countries and regions, Indigenous Peoples have experienced processes such as colonization, displacement, dispossession, forced cultural assimilation, discrimination and exclusion from decision-making. The specific experiences differ greatly between Peoples and places.

Important GHE principle

Learning about historical injustice should not reduce Indigenous Peoples to victims. Indigenous communities continue to demonstrate leadership, resilience, cultural continuity, innovation and self-determination.

STRENGTHS & RESILIENCE

Look beyond the deficit.

A health-equity approach should identify inequities while also recognizing strengths, assets, knowledge, leadership and community capacity.

01

Cultural continuity

Maintaining and revitalizing language, traditions, knowledge and cultural practices can support identity and connection.

02

Community leadership

Indigenous-led organizations and community leaders play important roles in shaping health and wellbeing.

03

Intergenerational knowledge

Knowledge shared across generations can strengthen resilience, identity and community capacity.

04

Youth leadership

Indigenous young people are important contributors to cultural continuity, advocacy, research and innovation.

EQUITY & SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH

Equity asks a deeper question.

It is not enough to ask whether everyone receives the same service. We must ask whether people have equitable opportunities to achieve health and wellbeing.

Who is more exposed to risk? Consider social, environmental and structural conditions.
Who has access to resources? Consider education, income, housing, food, land, transportation and healthcare.
Who makes decisions? Meaningful participation and self-determination matter.
Whose knowledge is heard? Health systems should listen to Indigenous knowledge holders, communities and lived experience.

THINK

Can you recognize the difference between a stereotype and evidence?

Good global health learning requires us to question assumptions rather than accepting simplified explanations about any population.

ASSUMPTION

"All Indigenous Peoples have the same culture."

This ignores the enormous diversity among Indigenous Peoples, nations, communities, languages and histories.

BETTER QUESTION

"Which people, place and history are we talking about?"

Context matters. Responsible health communication avoids treating diverse communities as one population.

GHE PRINCIPLE

Listen before you generalize.

Evidence should be combined with respectful attention to community context, lived experience and Indigenous voices.

GLOBAL HEALTH LEADERSHIP & EVIDENCE

Good Indigenous health work begins with listening.

Evidence matters. So do lived experience, community knowledge, Indigenous leadership and the context in which evidence is produced and applied.

Global Health Explorers should learn to ask not only "What does the evidence say?" but also:

Who produced the knowledge?
Whose voices are represented?
Who benefits from the intervention?
Was the community meaningfully involved?
Can the evidence be applied to this context?

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

10 Indigenous Health Questions

Think carefully. These questions test understanding of diversity, determinants, equity, culture, strengths and responsible global health thinking.

1. Which statement best describes Indigenous Peoples?

Correct answer: B. Indigenous Peoples are diverse populations with different nations, communities, languages, histories, cultures and contemporary realities. They should not be treated as one homogeneous group.

2. Why is culture relevant to Indigenous health?

Correct answer: C. Culture, identity, language, relationships, knowledge and connection can be important dimensions of wellbeing and can influence how health is understood and experienced.

3. Which is an example of a Social Determinant of Health?

Correct answer: D. Education, housing, income, environment, discrimination and access to healthcare can all influence health.

4. Why should Indigenous health not be described only through disadvantage?

Correct answer: A. A complete understanding should recognize inequities while also recognizing Indigenous strengths, resilience, cultural continuity, knowledge and leadership.

5. What does an equity approach ask?

Correct answer: C. An equity approach asks who faces greater risks, who has access to resources and who faces barriers to opportunities and services.

6. Why is history important when studying Indigenous health?

Correct answer: B. Historical and contemporary processes can shape living conditions, relationships with institutions, access to resources and health opportunities.

7. Which approach is most respectful when learning about an Indigenous community?

Correct answer: D. Learn about the specific community, listen to Indigenous voices, consider local context and avoid assuming that information about one Indigenous population applies to all.

8. Why does GHE connect Indigenous health from SELF to WORLD?

Correct answer: A. Health is influenced by interacting personal, family, community, national and global factors.

9. Which statement best reflects Indigenous health leadership?

Correct answer: C. Indigenous Peoples should be recognized as leaders, knowledge holders, researchers, practitioners and decision-makers rather than only as recipients of services.

10. What is one of the most important principles for a future health professional working with Indigenous communities?

Correct answer: B. Listen respectfully, understand the specific community context, avoid assumptions and work with Indigenous communities rather than simply designing solutions for them.
