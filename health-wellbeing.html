What does it mean to be healthy?

We often talk about health as if it were a single thing. But health is complex. People can experience different diseases, levels of functioning, access to care, quality of life and length of life.

That is why health researchers use many different measures. Each measure gives us a different piece of the picture.

💡
GHE PRINCIPLE

Health is multidimensional. No single number can tell us everything about the health and wellbeing of a person or population.

MEASURING HEALTH

Six ways to understand health

Health measurement uses different kinds of evidence to understand what is happening to people and populations.

01

Diseases & Health Conditions

We can measure how often diseases occur and how many people are affected.

ASK
How much disease is occurring, and among whom?
02

Health Indicators & Measures

Indicators can describe biological, clinical, functional, behavioural or population characteristics.

BMI · Blood pressure · HbA1c · DMFT · Immunization · Physical activity
ASK
What measurable characteristics describe health or risk?
03

Access to Health Services

Access includes more than having a healthcare facility nearby.

Physical · Financial · Social · Cultural · Availability · Acceptability · Quality
ASK
Can people obtain appropriate healthcare when they need it?
04

Health-Related Quality of Life

HRQoL considers how health affects everyday life and participation.

Physical functioning · Mental wellbeing · Pain · Social functioning · Daily activities
ASK
How does health affect how people live, function and participate?
05

Outcomes

Outcomes describe what happens to health, survival, functioning or disease over time.

Mortality · Morbidity · Disability · Premature mortality · Life expectancy
ASK
What ultimately happens to people's health and survival?
06

Impact Indicators

Impact asks whether changes actually make a meaningful difference to people and populations.

Better HALE · Reduced preventable deaths · Reduced disability · Better wellbeing
ASK
Did something actually make a meaningful difference?

🔎 NOTICE THE DIFFERENCE

Differences are evidence — not conclusions.

The graph shows measurable differences in HALE between Canadian provinces. But the graph alone does not tell us why those differences exist.

A good global health explorer does not immediately label one population as “healthy” and another as “unhealthy.” Instead, they ask better questions.

ASK: What social, economic, environmental, demographic and healthcare factors might help explain these differences?
IMPORTANT

A population can have a lower HALE than another population while still having a HALE that is substantially higher than a global reference. Always ask: “Compared with what?”

SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH

Health differences make us ask why.

Differences in health measures may reflect differences in living conditions, resources, opportunities, environments, social structures and access to care.

The goal is not simply to identify a difference. The goal is to understand the factors behind it.
