Why is bone health important?

Bones are living tissues. They provide structure, protect organs, support movement and store important minerals. Bone health therefore matters throughout the entire life course—not only when people become older.

During childhood and adolescence, the skeleton is growing rapidly. The amount and quality of bone built during these years can influence bone health later in life.

Osteoporosis is a disease in which bones become weaker and more fragile, increasing the likelihood of fractures. It often has no obvious symptoms until a fracture occurs. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

Fragility fractures can affect mobility, independence, education, work, family life and quality of life. Their prevention is therefore both a health issue and a social and economic issue. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Explore

Let's build a common language for understanding bones, osteoporosis and fracture prevention.

Bone

Living tissue that provides support, protects organs, enables movement and stores minerals.

Bone density

A measure related to the amount of mineral contained in bone. Lower bone density can be associated with greater fracture risk.

Osteoporosis

A disease characterized by reduced bone strength, making bones more fragile and more likely to break. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

Osteopenia

Bone density that is lower than the usual young-adult reference range but does not meet the diagnostic threshold for osteoporosis.

Fragility fracture

A fracture resulting from a force that would not normally break healthy bone, such as a fall from standing height.

Bone mineral density

A measurement used as part of assessing bone health and fracture risk in appropriate clinical settings.

Fracture risk

The probability that a person will experience a fracture, influenced by multiple clinical and environmental factors.

Healthy ageing

Supporting health, function, independence and participation across the life course.

04

Learn

Bones are living tissues

Bone is not an inert structure. Throughout life, old bone is removed and new bone is formed. This continuous process helps the skeleton adapt and maintain strength.

During childhood and adolescence, bone formation is particularly important because the skeleton is growing and developing.

Why adolescence matters

The teenage years are an important period for skeletal development. Adequate nutrition, regular physical activity and overall health support normal growth and development.

This does not mean that young people should be expected to manage all future osteoporosis risk themselves. Bone health is shaped by biology, living conditions, healthcare, education and broader social environments.

Nutrition and bone health

Calcium, vitamin D, protein and other nutrients contribute to healthy bones and normal body function.

Nutritional needs vary according to age, health status and other circumstances. Access to nutritious food is therefore an important population-health issue.

Movement and physical activity

Appropriate physical activity supports bones, muscles, balance and physical function.

For young people, active play, sport and other enjoyable forms of movement can contribute to healthy development. For older adults, maintaining strength, balance and mobility can also help reduce some risks associated with falls.

What is osteoporosis?

Osteoporosis weakens bone and increases the risk of fractures. It can affect people without obvious warning signs, which means that risk may not become apparent until a fracture occurs. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

Risk is influenced by many factors, including age, previous fractures, family history, certain medical conditions and medications, nutritional factors, physical inactivity and smoking.

Fragility fractures

Fragility fractures are an important consequence of osteoporosis. They can lead to pain, reduced mobility, disability and loss of independence.

WHO identifies fragility fractures as a global public health concern and emphasizes the importance of prevention, risk assessment and appropriate management. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

Falls and the environment

Fracture risk is influenced not only by bone strength but also by the likelihood of falling.

Lighting, stairs, flooring, housing design, transportation, accessibility and safe public spaces can therefore become part of a bone-health strategy.

Osteoporosis is not simply "an old person's disease"

Although osteoporosis and fragility fractures become more common with increasing age, bone health begins much earlier.

Healthy ageing starts across the life course. WHO notes that population ageing is occurring worldwide and that by 2050, around 80% of people aged 60 and older will live in low- and middle-income countries. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

Social Determinants of Health

Bone health and fracture outcomes are influenced by the conditions in which people are born, grow, learn, work, live and age.

  • Food security: access to nutritious food affects opportunities for adequate nutrition throughout life.
  • Income: household resources can influence food, housing, recreation, transportation and healthcare access.
  • Housing: safe, accessible housing can influence fall risk, independence and recovery after fractures.
  • Education: health literacy can influence people's ability to understand prevention, risk and treatment options.
  • Physical environment: safe streets, parks, schools and public spaces can support physical activity and mobility.
  • Healthcare access: timely assessment, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation can influence fracture outcomes.
  • Social support: family, peers and community networks can influence recovery, mobility and independence.
Equity question:

Can we expect the same bone-health outcomes from everyone when people do not have the same access to nutritious food, safe environments or healthcare?

Global Leadership & Evidence

Who leads bone-health action?

  • WHO — global work on musculoskeletal health, healthy ageing, rehabilitation and fragility-fracture prevention. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}
  • International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF) — global advocacy, education, evidence and action on osteoporosis and fragility fractures. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}
  • National and provincial health authorities — prevention, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation, surveillance and healthy-ageing programmes.
  • Primary healthcare services — identification of risk, appropriate assessment, referral, prevention and continuity of care.
  • Universities and scientific organizations — research on bone biology, fracture risk, prevention, treatment and rehabilitation.
  • Communities and civil society — advocacy for accessible environments, healthy ageing and equitable access to care.

Act

I can make healthy choices for myself, and I can help create healthier conditions for others.
  • Learn how bones grow and change across the life course.
  • Seek reliable information about nutrition, physical activity and bone health.
  • Support inclusive opportunities for safe, enjoyable physical activity.
  • Notice how housing, transportation and public spaces can influence mobility and safety.
  • Challenge stereotypes that portray osteoporosis or disability as simply an unavoidable consequence of ageing.
  • Learn why early recognition and appropriate healthcare can matter after a suspected fragility fracture.
  • Ask how health systems can make osteoporosis prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation more accessible.
  • Think globally: what would equitable bone health look like in countries with very different resources and health systems?
