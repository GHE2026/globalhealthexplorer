This GHE page is an educational introduction to first aid and emergency preparedness. It does not diagnose illness, prescribe treatment, or replace hands-on first-aid training, emergency services or professional healthcare.

01 · WHY THIS MATTERS

Emergencies can happen anywhere — preparedness helps us respond safely.

First aid is the immediate assistance provided when someone becomes ill or injured. Its purpose is not to turn a young person into a doctor. It is to help people recognize that something may be wrong, act safely, prevent further harm and connect the person with appropriate help.

The International Committee of the Red Cross describes first aid as essential assistance intended to preserve life, prevent further injury or illness, alleviate suffering and contribute to recovery.

This makes first aid both a health skill and a human responsibility: we look after ourselves, notice when others may need help, and understand when trained professionals should take over.

02 · THE GHE FIRST-AID PATHWAY

Recognize → Respond → Protect → Get Help → Recover

First aid begins before any technique. The first question is whether there is a problem, whether the situation is safe, and what kind of help is needed.

01
NOTICE

Recognize

Notice signs that someone may be injured, unwell, distressed or in danger.

  • Look for something unusual.
  • Listen to what the person says.
  • Notice changes in behaviour or responsiveness.
  • Take sudden or serious concerns seriously.
02
ACT

Respond

Stay calm and take an appropriate first step rather than rushing into an unsafe situation.

  • Stay calm.
  • Get the attention of a trusted adult when appropriate.
  • Follow your first-aid training.
  • Follow emergency-service instructions.
03
SAFETY

Protect

Protect yourself, the person who needs help and others nearby from additional danger.

  • Consider whether the scene is safe.
  • Avoid creating another emergency.
  • Respect privacy and dignity.
  • Use available protective equipment when appropriate.
04
CONNECT

Get Help

First aid is often a bridge between an emergency and professional care.

  • Know when emergency help is needed.
  • Contact local emergency services when appropriate.
  • Tell a trusted adult.
  • Give clear, useful information.
05
FOLLOW THROUGH

Recover

Recovery includes continued care, communication, learning and emotional support.

  • Follow professional advice.
  • Check that appropriate follow-up occurs.
  • Reflect on what was learned.
  • Support community preparedness.
03 · KNOW THE BOUNDARIES

First aid is not diagnosis — and it is not professional treatment.

FIRST AID

Immediate support

Safe, appropriate actions that can help someone while further assistance is being arranged or while waiting for professional care.

→
DIAGNOSIS

Clinical assessment

Determining the cause or nature of a health problem requires appropriate clinical knowledge, assessment and, when needed, testing.

→
PROFESSIONAL CARE

Treatment & follow-up

Healthcare professionals may provide assessment, treatment, monitoring, referral and ongoing care.

04 · BE PREPARED

Good first aid begins before an emergency happens.

Preparedness reduces uncertainty. Families, schools and communities can think ahead about risks, communication, emergency supplies and how people will support one another.

PLAN

Make a household plan

Know how your household will communicate, where people will meet, who can help children and what to do if normal services are disrupted.

KIT

Build an emergency kit

Keep essential supplies organized, accessible and adapted to the needs of your household.

  • Basic first-aid supplies
  • Essential medications and health equipment
  • Water, food and lighting
  • Important information and documents

First aid matters in homes, schools, communities — and humanitarian crises.

ICRC

First aid in an emergency

The ICRC's 2025 publication presents first aid as essential assistance intended to preserve life, prevent further injury or illness, alleviate suffering and contribute to recovery.

In humanitarian settings, first aid is connected to dignity, safety, protection and the realities of responding when resources may be limited.

IRC

Health in humanitarian emergencies

The International Rescue Committee works in humanitarian crises to help people survive, recover and regain control of their futures.

Its work reminds us that emergency health is not only about an individual event. Conflict, disaster, displacement, poverty, disrupted services and limited access to care can all shape health outcomes.

07 · FROM FIRST AID TO EMERGENCY CARE

WHO + ICRC: understanding the next level of care.

The World Health Organization and International Committee of the Red Cross developed Basic Emergency Care (BEC) as an open-access training programme for first-contact healthcare providers managing acute illness and injury, particularly where resources may be limited.

BEC uses a systematic approach to emergency assessment and management and addresses areas such as trauma, difficulty breathing, shock and altered mental status.

For GHE learners, the important lesson is not to reproduce clinical protocols. Instead, it is to understand how first aid connects to emergency care systems and why trained healthcare workers need structured approaches to serious illness and injury.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

BEC is not a youth procedural manual.

It is primarily a professional resource for first-contact healthcare providers.

08 · CHILDREN, FAMILIES & PREPAREDNESS

Preparedness should reflect the people who depend on the plan.

01

Different needs matter

Children, older adults, people with disabilities, people with chronic health needs and people who rely on medications or medical equipment may have additional preparedness needs.

02

Children can participate

Age-appropriate preparedness can help children understand what to do, who to contact and how adults and emergency services can help.

03

Plans should be practised

A plan is more useful when people know where it is, understand their roles and revisit it as household circumstances change.

04

Preparedness is shared

Families and communities become stronger when people consider accessibility, communication, social support and the needs of neighbours.

11 · SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH

Being prepared is easier when people have the resources to prepare.

Emergency preparedness is not equally easy for everyone. Housing, income, transportation, disability access, language, digital access, social support, healthcare availability and community infrastructure can shape a person's ability to prepare and respond.

A Global Health Explorer therefore asks not only: “Are people prepared?” but also: “Do people have a fair opportunity to become prepared?”
