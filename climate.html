Climate & Environment

How does a changing planet become a health issue?

Climate change is not only about temperature. It changes the environments in which people live, learn, work and grow — influencing air, water, food, livelihoods, infectious diseases, mental health and access to health services.

01 • UNDERSTAND

Climate Change: What Is Actually Changing?

Climate describes long-term patterns of temperature, precipitation, winds and other atmospheric conditions. Climate change refers to persistent changes in those patterns. Human activities — particularly the burning of fossil fuels — are the dominant driver of current warming.

🌡️

A warming climate

Rising average temperatures change the frequency, intensity and duration of heat extremes.

🌧️

Changing water patterns

Rainfall, drought, floods and water availability can change across regions.

🔥

Extreme events

Heatwaves, wildfires, storms, floods and droughts can create direct and indirect health risks.

🌿

Changing ecosystems

Climate pressures interact with biodiversity loss, land-use change and other environmental pressures.

1.44°C
A changing global climate

The United Nations reported in 2026 that the planet is already about 1.44°C warmer than in the late 1800s.

02 • CONNECT

Climate Change Becomes a Health Issue Through Pathways

Climate hazards do not affect health in only one way. They interact with environmental conditions, social circumstances, behaviours and health-system capacity.

1 Climate hazard Heat, flood, drought, wildfire, storm
→
2 Exposure Who or what is exposed?
→
3 Vulnerability Who has fewer protections?
→
4 Health outcome Illness, injury, stress or disruption

Extreme heat

Heat can worsen cardiovascular, respiratory and other health conditions and can increase the risk of heat-related illness.

Air quality

Climate-related heat, wildfire smoke and other conditions can contribute to harmful air pollution.

Water & food

Floods, droughts and changing environmental conditions can affect water security, food systems and foodborne disease risks.

Infectious diseases

Climate conditions can alter the distribution and transmission patterns of some vector-, food- and water-borne diseases.

Mental health

Disasters, displacement, loss of livelihoods and prolonged environmental stress can affect mental health and well-being.

Health systems

Climate hazards can damage facilities and infrastructure while increasing demand for care.

HEALTH EVIDENCE

Why Public Health Cares About Climate

3.6 billion

people already live in areas highly susceptible to climate change, according to WHO.

~250,000

additional deaths per year were estimated by WHO for 2030–2050 from selected climate-sensitive causes including malnutrition, malaria, diarrhoea and heat stress.

$2–4 billion

estimated direct annual health damage costs from climate change by 2030, excluding health-determining sectors such as agriculture and water and sanitation.

These are modelled estimates and should be interpreted in their stated context. Climate-health impacts are influenced by exposure, vulnerability, adaptation and future emissions.

03 • SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH

Climate Change Is Also an SDH Issue

Climate hazards do not affect everyone equally. Social and economic conditions shape exposure, vulnerability, access to protection and the ability to recover.

Climate hazard + Exposure + Vulnerability + Limited resources → Higher health risk

Income & poverty

Income affects housing quality, transportation, access to cooling, insurance, food security and the ability to recover after climate-related events.

Housing & neighbourhoods

Location, housing conditions, green space, heat exposure and infrastructure can shape environmental health risks.

Education & information

Access to reliable information and education can influence preparedness, risk perception and the ability to act on warnings.

Work & livelihoods

Outdoor workers, agricultural workers and others whose livelihoods depend on environmental conditions may face increased exposure.

Access to health services

Climate hazards can increase health needs while simultaneously disrupting transportation, facilities, electricity and supply chains.

Social support

Strong social networks and community organizations can help people prepare, respond and recover.

THINK DEEPLY

The Same Hazard Does Not Mean the Same Risk

Two communities can experience the same heatwave, flood or wildfire smoke event but have very different health outcomes.

Community A

  • Reliable cooling
  • Accessible health services
  • Strong early-warning systems
  • Safe housing
  • Financial resources
  • Strong community networks
Lower vulnerability
SAME HAZARD

Community B

  • Unsafe or overcrowded housing
  • Limited health-care access
  • Weak warning systems
  • Outdoor employment
  • Financial insecurity
  • Limited social protection
Higher vulnerability
GHE Data Question

If two places experience the same temperature increase, should we assume they experience the same health risk?

04 • CREATE SOLUTIONS

Mitigation + Adaptation

Climate action has two closely connected dimensions: reducing the causes of climate change and preparing for impacts that are already occurring or expected.

MITIGATION

Reduce the causes

Mitigation means reducing greenhouse-gas emissions or increasing their removal from the atmosphere.

  • Renewable energy
  • Energy efficiency
  • Cleaner transportation
  • Lower-carbon buildings
  • Sustainable food systems
  • Protection and restoration of ecosystems
ADAPTATION

Reduce vulnerability

Adaptation means changing decisions, systems and practices to reduce the health and social impacts of climate hazards.

  • Heat-warning systems
  • Climate-resilient health facilities
  • Flood preparedness
  • Water security
  • Climate-informed disease surveillance
  • Community resilience
HEALTH CO-BENEFITS

Climate action can also improve health today

Cleaner energy, active transportation, healthier urban environments and improved food systems can reduce emissions while producing health benefits, particularly through cleaner air and healthier living conditions.

CANADA • YOUR NATIONAL CONTEXT

Climate & Health in Canada

Climate risks vary across Canada's large and diverse geography. Health Canada identifies risks including extreme heat, floods, storms, air pollution, water quality and security, food security, infectious diseases and mental health impacts.

01

Extreme heat

Heat events can create significant risks, particularly for people who are more vulnerable.

02

Wildfire smoke

Smoke can affect air quality over large areas, sometimes far from the fire itself.

03

Infectious diseases

Changing environmental conditions can influence some climate-sensitive infectious diseases.

04

Water & food

Climate-related hazards can affect water quality, availability and food systems.

Health-system perspective

Adaptation is not simply an environmental task. Health authorities need climate information, preparedness plans, surveillance, resilient infrastructure and community partnerships.

GHE DATA DETECTIVE

Don't Just Look at the Climate Data — Ask Better Questions

A number becomes useful evidence only when we understand what it measures, who it represents, when it was collected and what comparison is appropriate.

01

What?

What variable is being measured?

02

Where?

What geographic area does the data represent?

03

When?

What time period does the measurement cover?

04

Who?

Which populations are represented — and who may be missing?

05

Compared with what?

Is the comparison using the same denominator, period and definition?

06

What does it mean?

What conclusion is supported — and what remains uncertain?
