HEALTHY LIVING · SUBJECT 2

Physical Activity
& Active Living

Moving, playing, learning and living actively — from ourselves to healthier families, communities and societies.

GLOBAL EVIDENCE

What does WHO recommend?

60 minutes

Average daily physical activity

For children and adolescents aged 5–17 years, WHO recommends an average of at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per day across the week.

Most activity should be aerobic. Vigorous-intensity aerobic activities, as well as activities that strengthen muscles and bones, should be incorporated at least 3 days per week.

Every move counts.

Physical activity includes active play, walking, cycling, sport, recreation, physical education, transportation, household activities and many forms of everyday movement.

EXPLORE

Why is this important?

Physical activity is an important part of healthy growth and development. Regular movement supports physical fitness and contributes to cardiovascular, metabolic, musculoskeletal and psychological health.

For children and adolescents, movement is also part of everyday life: playing, travelling to school, participating in physical education, spending time outdoors, enjoying recreation and connecting with friends.

But physical activity is not simply a matter of individual motivation. Whether young people can move safely and enjoyably is influenced by families, schools, neighbourhoods, transport, public spaces, economic circumstances, disability access, cultural expectations and many other social determinants of health.

LOOK AT THE EVIDENCE

Physical Inactivity Is a Global Health Issue

Global averages can reveal a major public health challenge — but they are only the beginning. Who is affected, where, and under what conditions matters too.

80%

About four in five adolescents do not meet recommended levels of physical activity.

85%

Adolescent girls

Globally, 85% of girls aged 11–17 were insufficiently active, compared with 78% of boys.

LEARN · YOUR BODY

What Happens When You Move?

Physical activity is a conversation between your muscles, heart, lungs, brain and many other systems. Your body responds to the demands placed on it.

♥

Your Heart

Working muscles need more oxygen and nutrients. Heart rate rises and the cardiovascular system increases blood flow to help meet the demand.

↑

Your Lungs

Breathing becomes faster and deeper so the body can bring in oxygen and remove carbon dioxide produced during metabolism.

≋

Your Temperature

Working muscles produce heat. Sweating and increased blood flow near the skin help the body regulate temperature.

THINK · ADAPTATION

Your Body Learns From What You Ask It To Do

SAID — Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demands

When the body repeatedly experiences a particular physical demand, it can adapt to become better able to handle that demand. The type of activity matters.

Strength

Repeated resistance movements can help muscles become better at producing force.

Example: practising controlled push-ups can improve the ability to perform push-ups.

Endurance

Gradually repeated aerobic activity can improve the body's ability to sustain activity.

Example: a gradual walk–run programme can help someone become more comfortable with running.

Recovery Matters

Adaptation does not mean that harder is always better. Rest, recovery, sleep, nutrition and variation are important parts of sustainable activity.

Specificity

To improve a particular physical skill, practice that skill. Swimming develops through swimming; cycling does not perfectly substitute for it.

But Variety Helps Too

Young people can benefit from experiencing different activities and movement skills rather than feeling pressured to specialize too early.

Progress Gradually

Sustainable improvement usually comes from appropriate progression rather than sudden large increases in training or activity.

LEARN · FITNESS

Four Components of Physical Fitness

Fitness is multidimensional. Different activities develop different capacities, and being fit is not the same thing as looking a particular way.

Cardiorespiratory Endurance

The ability of the heart, lungs and circulatory system to support sustained activity.

Examples include brisk walking, swimming, cycling and running.

Muscular Strength

The ability of a muscle or group of muscles to produce force.

Examples include resistance exercises, climbing and jumping.

Muscular Endurance

The ability of muscles to continue performing a task over time.

Everyday activities can develop endurance as well as structured exercise.

Flexibility & Mobility

The ability to move joints and body segments through an appropriate range of motion.

Mobility, control and comfortable movement all contribute to physical function.

CONNECT · WELLBEING

Movement, Mind and Social Connection

Physical activity is connected with mental wellbeing. WHO notes that regular activity can reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety and support brain health and overall wellbeing.

Movement can also create opportunities for friendship, recreation, time outdoors, participation and a sense of belonging.

But activity should not be presented as a cure for every emotional difficulty. Mental wellbeing is shaped by many biological, psychological and social factors.

THINK GLOBALLY · EQUITY

Who Gets the Chance to Be Active?

Physical activity is not distributed equally. Gender, socioeconomic circumstances, age, geography, disability, safety and access to facilities can all influence opportunities for movement.

Americas

35.6%

Of adults aged 18+ in the Americas were insufficiently active in 2022.

The estimate was 40.6% among women and 30.5% among men.

PAHO/WHO, 2024.

African Region

85%

More than four in five in-school adolescents in the African Region were insufficiently active.

About 22% of adults were also insufficiently active.

WHO Regional Office for Africa.

Europe & Central Asia

25% / 15%

In the 2021/2022 HBSC survey, only 25% of boys and 15% of girls reported 60 minutes of daily moderate-to-vigorous physical activity.

WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2024.

The important question is not simply: “How active are young people?”

It is also: “Which young people have fewer opportunities, and why?”

GLOBAL HEALTH DATA LITERACY

Don't Just Look at the Average

A population average can hide important differences. Global health explorers learn to ask who is represented inside the average.

Your WHO Inequality Challenge

Imagine that a country reports one national average for physical activity. Before accepting the number, ask:

01 Who is active?
02 Who is not?
03 Does age matter?
04 Does sex or gender matter?
05 Does economic status matter?
06 Does education matter?
07 Does place of residence matter?
08 Are disability and accessibility considered?
09 What environmental barriers exist?
10 What could reduce the gap?

This is the beginning of health inequality monitoring: using disaggregated data to understand who may be left behind and where action could make a difference.

ACT · CHANGE THE ENVIRONMENT

Healthy Movement Needs Healthy Environments

People cannot always choose their opportunities for movement. Communities and systems can make physical activity easier, safer and more inclusive.

Safe Streets

Walking and cycling become easier when streets, crossings and routes are designed with safety in mind.

Parks & Public Spaces

Accessible parks, recreation areas and public spaces can create opportunities for play and activity.

Schools

Physical education, active breaks, recreation and supportive school environments can help young people move throughout the day.

Transport

Walking, cycling and active transportation can contribute to health while reducing congestion and environmental impacts.

Inclusion

Active environments should consider disability, affordability, safety, culture, gender and different abilities.

Opportunity

The goal is not to make everyone exercise in the same way. It is to expand meaningful opportunities for people to move.
