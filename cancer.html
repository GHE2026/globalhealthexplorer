Why is cancer important?

Cancer is a major global health challenge. It is not one single disease, but a large group of diseases in which abnormal cells grow in ways that can interfere with the normal functioning of the body and may spread to other parts of the body.

According to WHO, cancer caused nearly 10 million deaths worldwide in 2024, making it one of the leading causes of death globally. The most common cancers include cancers of the lung, breast, colon and rectum, and prostate.

But cancer is not simply a matter of individual biology or personal choices. Risk is shaped by tobacco exposure, infections, environmental pollution, occupational exposures, radiation, access to prevention, early detection, diagnosis and treatment, and the social and economic conditions in which people live.

WHO and IARC reported in 2026 that approximately 37% of new cancer cases in 2022—around 7.1 million cases—were linked to causes that can potentially be prevented.

Look beyond the individual

Cancer risk is not distributed equally. People may experience different exposures to tobacco smoke, air pollution, occupational hazards, infections, ultraviolet radiation and other risk factors.

Access to vaccination, screening, diagnosis, treatment, medicines and supportive care also varies between communities and countries.

03

Explore

Let's build a common language for understanding cancer.

Cell

The basic structural and functional unit of the body. Cancer begins when changes within cells disrupt normal control of growth and behaviour.

Tumour

An abnormal mass of tissue. Tumours may be benign or malignant; not every tumour is cancer.

Malignant

Describes a cancerous growth that can invade surrounding tissues and, in some cases, spread to distant organs.

Metastasis

The spread of cancer cells from the original site to another part of the body.

Risk factor

A characteristic, exposure or condition associated with a greater likelihood of developing a disease.

Carcinogen

An agent or exposure that can cause cancer, such as tobacco smoke, certain forms of radiation or some occupational chemicals.

Prevention

Actions and policies that reduce exposure to cancer risks and help prevent cancer from developing.

Early detection

Finding cancer at an earlier stage when treatment may be more effective. Early detection includes awareness, timely diagnosis and, for selected cancers, screening.

04

Learn

What is cancer?

Cancer develops when abnormal cells grow and divide in an uncontrolled way. These cells can sometimes invade nearby tissues and spread to distant parts of the body.

Cancer develops through a complex interaction between genetic changes and environmental, behavioural and biological factors. There is therefore no single explanation for all cancers.

Can cancer be prevented?

Many cancers can be prevented or their risk reduced. WHO and IARC estimate that around four in ten cancer cases globally could potentially be prevented by addressing known preventable causes.

Important prevention strategies include reducing tobacco exposure, limiting harmful alcohol exposure, preventing certain infections through vaccination, reducing exposure to carcinogens, protecting against excessive ultraviolet radiation, and supporting healthier environments.

Prevention is therefore both a personal and a population-level responsibility.

Tobacco and cancer

Tobacco is one of the most important preventable causes of cancer. Tobacco exposure is associated with many cancers, including lung, oral, throat, oesophageal, stomach, bladder, pancreatic and several other cancers.

Protecting people from tobacco therefore requires more than asking individuals to make different choices. Smoke-free environments, regulation, taxation, health information and support for cessation are examples of population-level approaches.

Infections and cancer

Some infections can contribute to cancer. Important examples include human papillomavirus (HPV), hepatitis B virus and hepatitis C virus, as well as certain other infectious agents.

Vaccination, infection prevention, testing and appropriate treatment can therefore contribute to cancer prevention. HPV vaccination is particularly important because persistent infection with high-risk HPV types can cause cervical cancer and several other cancers.

Screening and early detection

Screening means testing people who do not necessarily have symptoms to identify disease or precancerous changes at an earlier stage.

Screening is not appropriate for every cancer or for every person at every age. Effective screening programmes require evidence, quality assurance, follow-up and access to diagnosis and treatment.

This is an important health-systems lesson: finding a possible cancer is only useful if people can receive appropriate follow-up and care.

Treatment and survivorship

Cancer treatment may include surgery, radiation therapy, medicines such as chemotherapy or targeted treatments, immunotherapy, hormone therapy, or combinations of different approaches.

Advances in diagnosis and treatment have improved outcomes for many cancers. However, access to high-quality treatment remains unequal between and within countries.

People who survive cancer may also need long-term follow-up, rehabilitation, psychological support and help returning to school, work and everyday life.

Palliative care

Palliative care aims to improve quality of life and relieve suffering for people with serious illness and their families.

It can be provided alongside cancer treatment and is not limited to the final days of life. Access to appropriate palliative care is an important part of compassionate and equitable cancer systems.

Young people and cancer

Cancer is much less common in children and adolescents than in adults, but young people can develop cancer and families can be profoundly affected by it.

Young people can also become important health advocates: learning about prevention, challenging misinformation, supporting inclusive communities and understanding how science and public policy influence cancer outcomes.

Social Determinants of Health

Cancer risk and outcomes are shaped by the conditions in which people are born, grow, learn, work, live and age.

  • Income and poverty: can influence housing, food environments, environmental exposures, transportation and access to healthcare.
  • Education: can influence access to health information, prevention opportunities and the ability to navigate health systems.
  • Housing and environment: can influence exposure to air pollution, indoor pollutants and other environmental risks.
  • Work: some occupations involve exposure to carcinogens, radiation, dusts, chemicals or other hazards.
  • Healthcare access: affects vaccination, screening, timely diagnosis, treatment, medicines, rehabilitation and palliative care.
  • Geography: people living in rural, remote or underserved areas may face additional barriers to specialized services.
  • Commercial determinants: industries and markets can influence exposure to products and environments associated with cancer risk.
Equity question:

If some communities face greater exposure to cancer risks and have less access to prevention and treatment, is giving everyone exactly the same resources enough to achieve health equity?

09

Global Leadership & Evidence

Who leads cancer action?

  • World Health Organization (WHO) — develops global guidance and supports countries in cancer prevention, early detection, treatment and palliative care.
  • International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) — generates international evidence on cancer causes, prevention, surveillance and burden.
  • WHO/IARC Global Cancer Observatory — provides global cancer incidence, mortality and prevalence data.
  • UNICEF and other UN agencies — contribute to vaccination, child and adolescent health, equity and health-system strengthening relevant to cancer prevention and care.
  • National and provincial health authorities — organize cancer prevention, screening, diagnosis, treatment, surveillance and public-health programmes.
  • Cancer registries and research institutions — generate data that help countries understand where cancer occurs, who is affected and how outcomes change.

Act

I can make healthy choices for myself, and I can help create healthier conditions for others.
  • Learn to recognize reliable sources of cancer information.
  • Challenge cancer myths and misinformation when you encounter them.
  • Support environments that protect people from tobacco smoke.
  • Learn why vaccination against cancer-causing infections can be important.
  • Learn about environmental and occupational factors that can contribute to cancer risk.
  • Support respectful, stigma-free communities for people living with or recovering from cancer.
  • Explore how governments can make prevention, diagnosis and treatment more accessible.
  • Ask how cancer research and health systems can better serve underserved populations.
