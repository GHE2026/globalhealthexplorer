Why is cancer important?

Cancer is one of the world's major health challenges. It is not one single disease, but a large group of diseases in which abnormal cells grow in ways that can interfere with normal body functions and, in some cases, spread to other parts of the body.

According to the World Health Organization, cancer caused nearly 10 million deaths worldwide in 2024. At the same time, many cancers can be prevented, detected earlier, treated effectively, or controlled for long periods.

Cancer therefore teaches us an important global-health lesson: health outcomes are shaped by biology, behaviour, environments, social conditions, policies and health systems.

03

Explore

Before we study cancer in depth, let's learn the language used by scientists and public-health professionals.

Cancer

A group of diseases involving abnormal cell growth that can invade surrounding tissues or spread to other parts of the body.

Tumour

An abnormal mass of tissue. Tumours may be benign or malignant; not every tumour is cancer.

Metastasis

The spread of cancer cells from the original site to another part of the body.

Risk factor

An exposure, behaviour, condition or characteristic associated with an increased likelihood of disease.

Prevention

Actions and policies that reduce the likelihood of disease developing.

Early detection

Finding cancer at an earlier stage, when treatment may be more effective.

Learn

How does cancer develop?

Cancer begins when cells acquire changes that disrupt normal controls over growth, division and survival. These changes can accumulate over time.

Different cancers have different causes and risk factors. There is rarely one simple explanation for why a particular person develops cancer.

Important preventable risks

  • Tobacco use and exposure to tobacco smoke
  • Alcohol use
  • Certain infections, including HPV and hepatitis viruses
  • Ultraviolet radiation
  • Air pollution
  • Some occupational exposures
  • Certain dietary and metabolic risk factors

Prevention is more than personal behaviour

Cancer prevention can involve vaccination, tobacco control, safer workplaces, cleaner air, healthy food environments, appropriate screening, public education, regulation and strong health systems.

This is why cancer is a global-health and public-health issue, not simply an individual-health issue.

Early detection and treatment

Some cancers can be detected early through appropriate screening or recognition of symptoms. Early diagnosis can improve treatment options and outcomes.

However, screening is not appropriate for every cancer or every age group. Public-health programmes should be based on evidence about benefits, harms, feasibility and equity.

Social Determinants of Health

Cancer risk and cancer outcomes are influenced by the conditions in which people are born, grow, learn, work, live and age.

  • Income: affects exposure to hazards and ability to access prevention and treatment.
  • Education: influences access to reliable health information and navigation of health systems.
  • Housing: can influence environmental exposures and living conditions.
  • Work: some occupations involve exposure to carcinogenic substances or conditions.
  • Environment: air pollution and other environmental hazards can influence cancer risk.
  • Healthcare access: determines whether prevention, diagnosis and treatment are available and affordable.
Equity question:

If two people receive the same health advice but have very different resources and environments, have they really been given the same opportunity to be healthy?

Global Leadership & Evidence

Who generates and uses cancer evidence?

  • WHO — global health guidance, cancer prevention and health-system policy.
  • IARC — international cancer research and assessment of carcinogenic hazards.
  • UNICEF — child and adolescent health, including vaccination and prevention-related work.
  • National ministries and cancer agencies — national prevention, surveillance and cancer-control programmes.
  • Provincial and local public-health authorities — implementation, surveillance and community action.
  • Universities and cancer research institutions — scientific discovery and evaluation.
