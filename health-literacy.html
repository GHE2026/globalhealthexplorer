HEALTH LITERACY

Find it. Understand it. Question it. Use it wisely.

Health literacy helps us make sense of health information, communicate with health professionals, navigate services, recognize misinformation, and make informed decisions.

ACCESS → UNDERSTAND → APPRAISE → USE

WHY THIS MATTERS

Health Literacy Is a Life Skill

Every day we encounter health information — from teachers, parents, doctors, social media, websites, advertisements, friends, news organizations and artificial intelligence. Health literacy helps us decide what to do with that information.

01

Find

Locate health information and services that are relevant to your question.

02

Understand

Make sense of words, numbers, instructions, risks, benefits and choices.

03

Appraise

Ask whether information is trustworthy, current, relevant and supported by evidence.

04

Use

Apply reliable information to decisions, conversations, services and responsible action.

WHO PERSPECTIVE

What Does Health Literacy Mean?

The World Health Organization describes health literacy as the personal knowledge and competencies that develop through everyday activities, social interaction and across generations.

These competencies are shaped by the organizations, systems and resources that allow people to access, understand, appraise and use health information and services.

Health literacy is not simply “knowing health facts.” It is the ability to participate intelligently and responsibly in decisions about health.
01

Personal

Skills and confidence to find, understand, evaluate and use information.

02

Professional

Health workers need communication skills that respond to different health-literacy needs.

03

Organizational

Services should make information understandable, accessible and actionable.

04

Systemic

Policies, communication environments and health systems can either enable or create barriers.

THINK BEFORE YOU TRUST

Six Questions for Any Health Claim

01

WHO?

Who created or shared this information?

02

WHAT?

What exactly is the claim being made?

03

EVIDENCE?

What evidence supports the claim?

04

CURRENT?

Is the information current enough for this question?

05

BIAS?

Could money, ideology, advertising, fear or another interest influence the message?

06

ACTION?

What action does the information encourage — and is that action appropriate?

DIGITAL HEALTH LITERACY

The Internet Changed the Health Information Environment

Health information can now reach millions of people within minutes. That creates enormous opportunities — but also creates new responsibilities.

Digital health literacy includes the ability to find, understand, evaluate and responsibly use health information in digital environments.

+

Opportunity

Access to high-quality information, services, education and global knowledge.

!

Misinformation

False or inaccurate information can spread quickly, especially when it is emotionally engaging.

?

Uncertainty

Scientific evidence changes. A responsible learner knows when uncertainty remains.

AI

Artificial Intelligence

AI can help organize information, but an AI-generated answer is not automatically accurate or appropriate.

HEALTH LITERACY IN THE AI ERA

Ask AI — But Keep Your Critical Thinking

Generative AI can summarize, explain and help organize information. It can also make mistakes, omit context, reproduce bias or present uncertain information with confidence.

01

Verify

Check important claims against authoritative sources.

02

Compare

Look for agreement or disagreement across credible sources.

03

Contextualize

Ask whether the information actually applies to the population and situation involved.

04

Escalate

For important personal health decisions, involve an appropriate qualified health professional.

SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH

Health Literacy Is Also About Equity

People do not encounter health information under equal conditions.

Language, education, disability, income, geography, culture, discrimination, digital access and trust in institutions can influence whether someone can find, understand and use health information.

Therefore, improving health literacy cannot mean simply telling people to “try harder.” Health systems, schools, governments and organizations also have a responsibility to communicate clearly and reduce unnecessary barriers.

MEASURE

How Can Health Literacy Be Measured?

Researchers use validated instruments to study health literacy. A questionnaire is not the same thing as a diagnosis, and results should be interpreted carefully.

INSTRUMENT 01 MULTIDIMENSIONAL

Health Literacy Questionnaire

HLQ

The HLQ was developed as a multidimensional measure of health-literacy needs and strengths. It examines areas such as access to social support, ability to engage with health professionals, ability to actively manage health, and ability to find and understand health information.

9 domains
44 items
Validated measure
GHE note: Use the original instrument and its official scoring/interpretation guidance rather than copying or modifying individual items without permission.
View the original validation study →
INSTRUMENT 02 QUICK SCREEN

Newest Vital Sign

NVS

The NVS is a brief health-literacy screening instrument based on interpretation of a nutrition label and related questions. It assesses aspects of reading comprehension and numeracy relevant to health information.

6 questions
~3 minutes
Global adaptations
Canada: A Canadian adaptation has been validated in English and French and studied in Canadian populations.
View the Canadian validation study →
Important research principle:

A school or community project should never label classmates as “healthy,” “unhealthy,” “smart,” “poorly literate,” or “low health literacy” based on a classroom exercise. Validated instruments should be used only with appropriate supervision, consent/assent, privacy protections, and proper interpretation.

GHE EXERCISE 01

Health Information Detective

Learn to distinguish a trustworthy health claim from an attractive but weak one.

Your Mission

  1. Choose one common health question.
  2. Find three sources that discuss it.
  3. Identify who produced each source.
  4. Record the date and type of evidence.
  5. Compare the claims.
  6. Identify at least one uncertainty or limitation.
  7. Decide which source you would trust most — and explain why.
QUESTION SOURCE EVIDENCE TRUST?
What is the health claim? Who published it? What supports it? Why?
What does another source say? Is it independent? Do the findings agree? What remains uncertain?

GHE EXERCISE 02 · YOUNG RESEARCHERS

Measure Health Literacy in Your Community

With teacher, school-health professional, researcher, or healthcare-provider supervision, students can design a small health-literacy project.

This is a supervised educational research exercise. Do not collect names, diagnoses, medical histories, passwords, contact information, or other unnecessary personal information. Follow your school's research, privacy and consent requirements.
01

Define the Question

Example: “How confident are students in finding and evaluating trustworthy health information?”

02

Choose the Population

For example, one class, a supervised youth group, or another appropriate community sample.

03

Choose a Tool

With supervision, consider an established instrument such as the HLQ or NVS and follow its official administration requirements.

04

Protect Participants

Obtain appropriate consent/assent and keep responses private. Collect only what is necessary.

05

Analyze Carefully

Use simple descriptive statistics such as counts, percentages, means where appropriate, and patterns across groups only when ethically justified.

06

Do Not Diagnose

A classroom survey is not a clinical assessment and should not be used to label individual participants.

07

Share Findings

Present aggregate findings using graphs, tables or an infographic without identifying individuals.

08

Recommend Action

Identify one practical way your school or community could make health information easier to find, understand or use.

Suggested Final Product

Research question → Method → Participants → Findings → Limitations → Equity considerations → Recommendation → Reflection

GHE EXERCISE 03

Make Health Information Easier to Understand

Find a real health information page intended for the public. With your teacher or mentor, examine how easy it is to understand.

01

02

03

04

05

06

GLOBAL HEALTH

One Concept — Many Regions, Many Contexts

Health literacy is a global issue. Different WHO regions have developed and supported work addressing communication, health information, measurement, digital health and equity.

WHO

Global

WHO emphasizes access, understanding, appraisal and use of health information and services, while recognizing organizational and systemic responsibilities.

PAHO

Americas

PAHO highlights digital health literacy, digital inclusion and the need to avoid widening existing inequalities through digital transformation.

EMRO

Eastern Mediterranean

WHO EMRO connects health education, health promotion, communication and community capacity with stronger health literacy.

AFRO

Africa

WHO AFRO has supported population health-literacy measurement, including a national health-literacy survey in Liberia.

EURO

Europe

WHO/Europe's M-POHL network supports international measurement and improvement of population and organizational health literacy.
