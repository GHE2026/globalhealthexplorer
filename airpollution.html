01

Why is clean air important?

We breathe thousands of times every day. The air entering our lungs can contain gases, tiny particles, smoke, dust and other substances.

Air pollution can affect the lungs, heart, brain and other parts of the body. It is linked with diseases including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, heart disease and lung cancer.

Air pollution is not only an individual problem. The quality of the air around us is influenced by transportation, energy production, industry, agriculture, household fuels, waste, fires and the way communities are planned.

Explore

Let's learn the language scientists and public-health professionals use to describe air pollution.

Air pollution

Air pollution means that harmful chemical, physical or biological substances are present in indoor or outdoor air.

Indoor air pollution

Pollution inside homes, schools, workplaces and other buildings.

Outdoor air pollution

Pollution in the air around us, often influenced by traffic, industry, energy production, fires and other sources.

PM₂.₅

Very tiny particles that are 2.5 micrometres or smaller in diameter.

Think: very, very small particles.

PM₁₀

Particles that are 10 micrometres or smaller in diameter.

Think: small particles, including some dust.

Ozone (O₃)

A gas that can be harmful to breathe when it forms near the ground. It is different from the protective ozone layer high in the atmosphere.

Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂)

A gas commonly associated with burning fuels, including emissions from vehicles and other combustion sources.

Carbon monoxide (CO)

A colourless and odourless gas produced when fuels do not burn completely.

Sulfur dioxide (SO₂)

A gas released mainly by burning sulfur-containing fuels and some industrial processes.

04

Learn

Indoor and outdoor pollution

Air pollution can be found both inside and outside buildings.

Indoor sources can include smoke from cooking or heating with polluting fuels, tobacco smoke, some chemicals, dust, mould and poor ventilation.

Outdoor sources can include traffic, industry, power generation, construction, agriculture, waste burning and wildfire smoke.

Why are tiny particles important?

Some particles in polluted air are so small that we cannot see them.

PM₁₀ includes particles up to 10 micrometres across. PM₂.₅ includes even smaller particles, up to 2.5 micrometres across.

The smaller size of PM₂.₅ allows these particles to travel deep into the lungs. Fine particulate pollution is therefore an important public-health concern.

A SIMPLE COMPARISON

A micrometre is one millionth of a metre. A human hair is much wider than a PM₂.₅ particle. That is why these particles are often invisible.

Air pollution and health

Air pollution can affect people throughout life. Children, pregnant people, older adults and people living with chronic diseases may be particularly vulnerable to some effects.

Health effects can include respiratory symptoms, asthma exacerbations, cardiovascular disease, stroke, chronic respiratory disease and cancers.

The health impact depends on the pollutant, concentration, duration of exposure, individual susceptibility and the surrounding social and environmental conditions.

Household air pollution

In many parts of the world, families still cook or heat their homes using fuels and technologies that produce large amounts of smoke.

WHO reports that around 2.1 billion people were still cooking with open fires or inefficient stoves using fuels such as wood, charcoal, crop waste, dung, coal or kerosene in 2025.

This is not simply a question of personal choice. Access to affordable clean energy, infrastructure, income and public policy all matter.

Wildfire smoke

Wildfires can produce smoke containing particulate matter and other pollutants. Smoke can travel long distances, meaning that communities far from the fire may also experience poor air quality.

Wildfire smoke also shows why climate, environment, emergency preparedness and health cannot be treated as completely separate subjects.

Clean air is a systems issue

Air quality is influenced by choices made by households, businesses, cities and governments.

Cleaner transportation, cleaner energy, better urban planning, safer industrial processes, improved household energy and effective regulation can all contribute to healthier air.

05

How is air pollution measured?

Scientists use instruments to find out what is in the air and how much of it is present.

01

Concentration

Scientists measure how much of a pollutant is present in a certain amount of air.

02

µg/m³

This means micrograms per cubic metre.

In simple words: it tells us how much pollution is found in a particular volume of air.

03

Monitoring stations

Special instruments can continuously or regularly measure pollutants at selected locations.

04

Air Quality Index

Some governments combine pollution measurements into an easier-to-understand air-quality scale.

The exact scale and categories can differ between countries and jurisdictions.

What does PM₂.₅ = 20 µg/m³ mean?

It means that the measured air contains an average concentration of about 20 micrograms of PM₂.₅ in each cubic metre of air during the specified measurement period.

The number is not a percentage and it does not mean that every breath contains exactly the same amount. Air pollution changes over time and from place to place.

Why do measurements matter?

Measurements help scientists understand exposure, help health authorities identify pollution problems, and help governments evaluate whether policies are improving air quality.

Social Determinants of Health

The air people breathe is shaped by the conditions in which they live, learn, work and travel.

  • Income: influences housing, energy choices, transportation and exposure to environmental risks.
  • Housing: affects ventilation, dampness, mould, heating and indoor pollution.
  • Location: some communities may be closer to major roads, industrial facilities or other pollution sources.
  • Energy access: access to affordable clean household energy can reduce household air pollution.
  • Work: some occupations involve exposure to dust, fumes or other pollutants.
  • Urban planning: transport systems, housing and land use can influence population exposure.
  • Healthcare access: pollution-related illness may be harder to prevent or treat where healthcare is difficult to access.
Equity question:

Is it fair to tell everyone to "avoid polluted air" when some people have much less control over where they live, work or travel?

Global Leadership & Evidence

Who helps understand and reduce air pollution?

  • WHO — develops health-based air-quality guidelines, synthesizes evidence and supports countries on health and air pollution.
  • UN Environment Programme (UNEP) — works on environmental policy and global environmental action.
  • National and provincial/state authorities — monitor air quality, regulate emissions and implement public-health measures.
  • Local governments — influence transportation, land use, waste management, urban planning and local environments.
  • Universities and scientific institutions — measure pollution, study health effects and develop new methods and technologies.
  • Communities and civil society — can monitor local concerns, advocate for healthier environments and participate in environmental decision-making.
