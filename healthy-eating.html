WHY FOOD MATTERS
A foundation of health

Food is fundamental to health.

Healthy eating is about adequacy, balance, moderation and diversity. It is also about whether people have the knowledge, resources, opportunities and environments needed to make healthy choices possible.

Can I make informed choices?

Understanding food, nutrients, patterns, labels and evidence helps us think more clearly about what we eat.

Does everyone have a fair opportunity?

Healthy eating is shaped not only by individual choices, but also by family, community, economic, environmental and food-system conditions.

PERSONAL HEALTH HINTS

Small ways to think about healthy eating.

Healthy eating develops through understanding, opportunity and everyday decisions — not through perfection or comparison.

Variety

Include a range of foods and food groups over time.

Balance

Think about the overall pattern of eating, rather than one food or one meal.

Fibre

Foods rich in fibre can contribute to digestive and overall health.

Food Processing

Learn what different levels and types of food processing mean.

Sugar

Notice sources of added and free sugars without turning food into a source of guilt.

Regular Opportunities

Consistent opportunities to eat can support learning, energy and wellbeing.

Energy & Concentration

Food supports the energy and concentration needed for everyday life and learning.

Food & Feelings

Food, emotions, routines and social experiences can influence one another.

Food Environment

Availability, affordability, marketing and access influence what choices are possible.

One Small Improvement

Focus on one realistic, positive improvement rather than expecting perfection.

Healthy eating is not about perfection, restriction, body shape, appearance or comparison. It is about health, nourishment, knowledge, opportunity and sustainable choices.

DATA LITERACY

Don't Just Accept the Number.

Nutrition statistics can be powerful — but numbers need context, definitions, sources and critical thinking.

Your data challenge

When you see a nutrition statistic, stop and investigate before accepting it as fact.

  1. What exactly is being measured?
  2. Who collected the data?
  3. Who was included — and who was not?
  4. When and where was the information collected?
  5. Is the number a count, percentage, rate or estimate?
  6. What comparison or denominator is being used?
  7. What does the number actually tell us?
A number is evidence only when we understand what the number represents.
