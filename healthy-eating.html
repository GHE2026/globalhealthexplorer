WHY IS THIS IMPORTANT?

Food is fundamental to health.

Food is fundamental to growth, development, learning and wellbeing. WHO guidance describes a healthy diet through four core principles: adequacy, balance, moderation and diversity.

Healthy dietary practices established during childhood and adolescence can contribute to health across the life course.

But healthy eating is more than knowing which foods are nutritious. What we eat can be influenced by family, culture, income, time, school, food environments, advertising and the wider food system.

Two questions matter:

How can I make informed choices for my own health?

How can we create conditions in which everyone has a fair opportunity to eat well?

START WITH YOURSELF

🌱 Personal Health Hints

Take a moment to think about your everyday eating. There are no “perfect” answers. The goal is to notice, learn and make informed choices.

01

Variety

Do I eat a variety of foods? Different foods provide different nutrients.

02

Balance

Does my usual food pattern have a reasonable balance? Think about protein, carbohydrates, healthy fats, fibre, vitamins and minerals.

03

Fibre

Do I regularly eat foods rich in fibre? Vegetables, fruits, whole grains, beans, lentils, nuts and seeds can provide fibre.

04

Highly Processed Foods

How often do highly processed foods become my easiest choice? Convenience matters, but balance matters too.

05

Sugary Drinks

How often do I drink beverages with added sugars? Water and unsweetened drinks can support healthy hydration.

06

Regular Opportunities

Do I usually have regular opportunities to eat? School schedules, activities and family circumstances can influence eating patterns.

07

Food & Energy

Do I notice how different foods affect my energy and concentration?

08

Food & Feelings

Do stress, boredom, celebrations or emotions sometimes influence what I eat?

09

Food Environment

How easy is it for me to access nutritious food? What makes healthy food easier or harder for me?

10

One Small Improvement

If I wanted to make one realistic improvement, what would it be?

💚 Remember

Healthy eating is not about perfection, restriction, body shape, appearance or comparing yourself with others.

It is about nourishing your body, developing informed habits, enjoying food, respecting cultural and family food traditions, and understanding the circumstances that make healthy choices easier or harder.

Building Nutrition Literacy

Healthy Diet

Adequacy, balance, moderation and diversity.

Nutrients

Carbohydrates, protein, fats, fibre, vitamins, minerals and water.

Food Patterns

Fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds and protein-rich foods.

Food Processing

Understanding minimally processed and highly processed foods without simplistic “good/bad” labels.

Food Security

Understanding reliable access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food.

Malnutrition

Undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, overweight and obesity.

Food Safety

Why safe food handling, preparation and storage matter.

Food Environment

How availability, affordability, marketing and accessibility shape choices.

Who Is Working on Global Nutrition?

Global nutrition requires cooperation across health, food, agriculture, poverty, education and humanitarian systems.

WHO

Nutrition guidance, health consequences, monitoring and policy.

UNICEF

Child and adolescent nutrition, food poverty and equity.

FAO

Agriculture, food systems, food security and nutrition data.

WFP

Food security and humanitarian nutrition.

IFAD

Rural development and sustainable food systems.

World Bank

Poverty, human capital and nutrition financing.

Don't Just Accept the Number

🌍 The Challenge

Global estimates tell us that billions of people cannot afford a healthy diet.

01 What does “cannot afford” actually mean?
02 Is this the same as being hungry?
03 How is this measured?
04 Which populations might be most affected?
05 Has the situation changed over time?
06 Which SDGs are involved?
07 Who collected the evidence?
GHE Evidence Literacy Principle

Never accept a statistic without asking: Who measured it? When? How? Compared with what?

Making Responsible Generations for Global Health

These are complementary responsibilities. One does not imply that an individual's choices cause the conditions affecting others.
