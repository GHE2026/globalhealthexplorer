Why are NCDs important?

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) are health conditions that are not usually passed directly from one person to another.

The major groups include cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory diseases and diabetes.

Together, these diseases affect millions of families around the world. They can develop over many years, but the conditions that shape our health often begin much earlier in life.

NCDs are not simply the result of individual choices. The food available to people, the air they breathe, opportunities for physical activity, housing, education, income, working conditions, marketing, healthcare access and public policies can all influence health.

43 million+ NCD deaths globally in 2021
4 major groups Heart disease & stroke, cancer, chronic respiratory disease and diabetes
Shared risks Tobacco, unhealthy diets, inactivity, harmful alcohol use and air pollution

04

Learn

NCDs form a connected system

NCDs may appear to be separate diseases, but many share biological, environmental, social and economic pathways.

For example, unhealthy environments and patterns of exposure can contribute to raised blood pressure, high blood glucose and other metabolic changes. These can increase the risk of several NCDs.

Risk factors are not all the same

Some risk factors involve behaviours or exposures. Others are biological or metabolic. Still others arise from the social, commercial, physical and policy environments.

Living conditions → Exposures & opportunities → Risk factors → Health outcomes

Prevention is more than personal choice

Prevention can include individual actions, supportive families, healthier schools, safer workplaces, cleaner air, healthier food systems, effective regulation, accessible healthcare and evidence-informed public policy.

Early life matters

Health-related knowledge, environments, habits, opportunities and exposures during childhood and adolescence can influence health across the life course.

This is why GHE focuses on young people: learning about health today can help shape healthier societies tomorrow.

The Shared Risk-Factor Web

Many NCDs have overlapping risk factors. Understanding these connections helps us move from disease-by-disease thinking to population health.

01

Hypertension

Blood pressure that remains too high can damage blood vessels and increase the risk of heart disease and stroke.

02

Healthy Eating & Nutrition

Food systems, affordability, availability, culture and dietary patterns all influence health.

03

Physical Activity

Opportunities to move are shaped not only by motivation but also by safe spaces, schools, transport and neighbourhood design.

04

Tobacco & Smoke Exposure

Tobacco use and second-hand smoke are important causes of cardiovascular, respiratory and cancer-related disease.

05

Alcohol

Alcohol consumption can contribute to several health harms. Public health also considers the environments and policies that influence exposure.

06

Air Pollution

Indoor and outdoor air pollution can affect the lungs, heart and wider population health.

07

Weight & Metabolic Health

Weight is one part of metabolic health. GHE focuses on healthy environments and avoids blaming people for body size.

08

Social & Commercial Conditions

Advertising, income, education, housing, work, urban design and access to services can shape health opportunities.

Hypertension

High blood pressure is often called a "silent" risk factor because people may not know they have it. It is strongly connected with cardiovascular disease and stroke.

Healthy eating

Healthy eating is not about a perfect diet. It involves access to safe, affordable, nutritious and culturally appropriate foods.

Physical activity

Movement supports physical and mental well-being. Whether people can be active is influenced by schools, neighbourhoods, transport, safety and opportunities for recreation.

Tobacco and smoke exposure

Tobacco harms many organs and contributes to cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease and multiple cancers. Protecting people also means reducing exposure to second-hand smoke.

Alcohol

Alcohol is a population-health issue because consumption can contribute to disease and injury. Young people should understand the evidence, social influences and health consequences rather than receiving messages based on blame.

Air pollution

Fine particles and other pollutants can affect respiratory and cardiovascular health. Sources include traffic, industry, household fuels, wildfire smoke and other environmental exposures.

Weight and metabolic health

Metabolic health includes measures such as blood pressure, blood glucose and blood lipids. Body weight is influenced by biology, environment, food systems, activity opportunities and many other factors.

GHE does not promote body comparison or appearance ideals. The focus is health, dignity and supportive environments.

Social and commercial conditions

Health is influenced by the conditions in which people are born, grow, learn, work, live and age, as well as by commercial and policy environments.

Social Determinants of Health

NCDs cannot be understood only through biology. They are also shaped by the social, economic, commercial and physical environments in which people live.

Income & social protection

Economic resources influence food, housing, transportation, education and healthcare access.

Education

Education can influence health literacy, opportunities and the ability to navigate health systems.

Food environments

Availability, affordability, marketing and cultural context shape food choices.

Built environment

Streets, parks, transport, housing and neighbourhood safety can influence activity and exposure.

Work

Working conditions, income, stressors and occupational exposures can affect health.

Healthcare

Prevention, screening, diagnosis, medicines, treatment and continuity of care all matter.

Commercial determinants

Commercial practices, marketing and product environments can influence population exposure and behaviour.

Environment & climate

Air pollution, heat, wildfire smoke and other environmental changes can influence NCD risk.

Equity question

If two young people have different opportunities to eat well, move safely, breathe clean air and access healthcare, should we expect identical health outcomes?

Global Leadership & Evidence

Who works on NCDs?

  • WHO — global leadership, evidence, guidance, monitoring and support for NCD prevention and control.
  • UN agencies — address health alongside poverty, food systems, environment, development and inequality.
  • World Bank — financing, health-system strengthening and development analysis relevant to NCDs.
  • IHME — generates estimates of disease burden, risk factors and trends.
  • National and provincial health authorities — prevention, surveillance, regulation, screening and healthcare.
  • Universities and scientific organizations — research, innovation, surveillance and evaluation.
  • Communities and young people — advocacy, participation, innovation and accountability.

Act

I can make healthy choices for myself, and I can help create healthier conditions for others.
  • Learn to distinguish reliable health evidence from misinformation.
  • Encourage supportive and inclusive environments for physical activity.
  • Learn about healthy and culturally appropriate food environments.
  • Support smoke-free environments and policies that protect people from tobacco exposure.
  • Learn why clean air is a public-health issue.
  • Challenge stigma toward people living with chronic diseases or mental-health conditions.
  • Ask how schools and communities can become healthier environments.
  • Use evidence to participate in conversations about health policy and equity.
