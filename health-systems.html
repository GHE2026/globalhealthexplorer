What is a health system?

Think beyond hospitals and doctors. A health system includes the organizations, people, institutions, policies, financing arrangements, technologies and information systems that work together to improve health and respond to health needs.

🏛️

Institutions

Ministries, local authorities, hospitals, clinics, laboratories, public-health agencies, universities, communities and other organizations.

👩‍⚕️

People

Health workers, caregivers, managers, researchers, patients, families, community leaders and many others.

💰

Resources

Money, medicines, equipment, infrastructure, information, technology and time.

📜

Rules & policies

Laws, regulations, standards, benefit packages, professional rules and policies shape how the system works.

🤝

Relationships

Health systems depend on coordination among governments, professionals, communities, civil society and private organizations.

📊

Outcomes

The ultimate question is not simply how much a system spends, but what happens to people's health, well-being, experience and financial security.

WHO FRAMEWORK

The six building blocks

WHO uses six core components to describe health systems. They are connected: weakness in one area can affect performance in another.

Financing determines how resources are raised, pooled and spent — and whether people face financial hardship when seeking care.

Ask: Who pays, how much, and who is protected?

🧑‍⚕️

Health Workforce

Doctors, nurses, pharmacists, community health workers, technicians, managers and many other workers keep systems functioning.

Ask: Are enough trained people where they are needed?
05
💊

Quality, safe, effective and affordable medicines, vaccines, diagnostics and technologies must reach the people who need them.

Ask: Are essential products available, affordable and trustworthy?
06
📡

Health Information Systems

Health systems need reliable information to understand problems, monitor performance, detect threats and make better decisions.

Ask: What do we know — and what are we missing?
SYSTEMS THINKING

A health system is a system — not six separate boxes

Imagine a clinic with excellent doctors but no essential medicines. Or a country with medicines available in the capital but few health workers in remote communities.

A single intervention can fail when the surrounding system is not prepared to support it.

Systems thinking asks us to look for connections, feedback loops, bottlenecks, incentives and unintended consequences.

THE FRONT DOOR OF THE SYSTEM

Why Primary Health Care matters

Primary health care is not simply the first clinic someone visits. WHO describes it as a whole-of-society approach that brings integrated services closer to communities, addresses broader determinants of health, and empowers people and communities.

01

Integrated care

Promotion, prevention, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation and palliative care can be connected across the life course.

02

Community connection

Services work better when communities can help identify needs and shape solutions.

03

Wider determinants

Health systems cannot improve health sustainably while ignoring housing, education, food, work, environment and social protection.

04

Resilience

Strong primary health care can help systems prepare for, respond to and recover from shocks.

+
GHE systems question

If a health service is technically excellent but people cannot afford it, reach it, understand it, trust it or access it when needed — is the system truly working for everyone?

UHC

Universal Health Coverage

Universal health coverage means people can obtain the quality health services they need, when and where they need them, without financial hardship.

✓

Access

Services are available and reachable.

★

Quality

Services are safe, effective and appropriate.

🛡

Financial Protection

Seeking care should not cause financial hardship.

UHC is not “free everything.” It is a way of organizing coverage, services and financial protection so that people can obtain needed care without being pushed into financial hardship.

Governance: who decides?

Governance is about the structures, institutions, rules and processes through which health systems are directed and overseen.

Good governance is not simply about having a powerful ministry. It includes strategic direction, regulation, transparency, participation, accountability and the ability to coordinate different actors.

HEALTH WORKFORCE

People are the system

Buildings and technologies do not provide care by themselves. Health systems depend on people with different skills working together.

Workforce planning includes education, recruitment, distribution, retention, supervision, working conditions, professional development and leadership.

A country can have enough health workers overall while still having major shortages in particular communities, specialties or rural areas.

DATA → DECISION → ACTION

Can a health system learn?

Strong health systems need information that is timely, accurate, relevant and usable. Information should help people identify problems, allocate resources, monitor performance and improve care.

01 Measure Collect data
02 Analyse Find patterns and gaps
03 Interpret Understand context
04 Decide Choose an action
05 Learn Monitor what happened
Data literacy matters. An average can hide inequality. A national indicator can hide local gaps. A missing value can look like zero. And correlation does not automatically establish causation.
RESILIENCE

What happens when the system is shocked?

Pandemics, climate events, conflict, economic disruption, supply interruptions and other crises can expose weaknesses that were already present.

⚡

Prepare

Identify risks, maintain essential functions and plan for different scenarios.

🔎

Detect

Use surveillance and information systems to detect problems early.

🤝

Respond

Coordinate people, resources, communication and services quickly.

🔄

Recover

Restore services while learning from what happened.

🌱

Adapt

Change policies, infrastructure and practices based on evidence and experience.

SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH · SDH

Health systems cannot treat every determinant alone

Health systems are themselves part of the social environment. They can reduce or reproduce inequalities depending on how services, resources and opportunities are organized.

LOCAL

Community

Are services physically reachable? Are they culturally and socially acceptable? Can people participate in decisions affecting their health?

NATIONAL

Country

How do financing, education, employment, housing, social protection, laws and health policies shape access and health outcomes?

GLOBAL

World

How do global financing, medicines, trade, migration, climate change, technology, knowledge and international cooperation influence health systems?

SOCIAL CONDITIONS → HEALTH SYSTEM ACCESS → CARE & EXPERIENCE → HEALTH OUTCOMES

WHO emphasizes that the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age — together with access to power, money and resources — strongly influence health inequities.

HEALTH EQUITY

Same service does not always mean same opportunity

Imagine two communities. Both receive one new clinic. One already has transportation, internet access, stable housing and many health workers. The other has long travel distances, language barriers and fewer providers.

Counting clinics alone would miss important differences in the conditions needed to benefit from care.

Equality

The same resource or service is provided.

Equity

Resources and approaches respond to differences in need and barriers.

GHE data question: When comparing health-system performance, ask not only “What is the national average?” but also “Who is doing better, who is doing worse, and what might explain the difference?”

GHE DATA DETECTIVE

Before comparing two health systems

Strong comparisons require more than finding two numbers. Use these six checks.

01 Same definition?

Are both indicators measuring the same thing?

02 Same year?

Older and newer data may not be directly comparable.

03 Same population?

Age, sex, geography and other characteristics matter.

04 Same denominator?

Rates, proportions and counts answer different questions.

05 Average or distribution?

National averages can hide differences between groups.

06 Association or cause?

A relationship between two indicators does not by itself prove causation.
