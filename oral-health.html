Oral Health

Your mouth is part of your health. Explore teeth, gums, prevention, evidence, equity and the systems that shape oral health around the world.

EXPLORE

Why is Oral Health Important?

Oral health is much more than having clean teeth. A healthy mouth helps us eat, speak, breathe, communicate and participate in everyday life.

Oral diseases are among the most common health conditions worldwide. Many are preventable, yet prevention and treatment are not equally available to everyone.

That makes oral health both a personal health issue and a global public-health issue.

GHE QUESTION

If many oral diseases are preventable, why do they remain so common?

The answer takes us beyond individual behaviour into food environments, education, income, prevention, health services and public policy.

GLOBAL EVIDENCE

Teeth, Gums & the Oral Microbiome

T

Teeth

Enamel protects the tooth. Beneath it are dentin and the living tissues of the tooth. Teeth help us bite, chew and speak.

G

Gums

Gums and supporting tissues help hold teeth in place. Plaque around the gumline can contribute to inflammation.

M

Oral Microbiome

The mouth contains many microorganisms. Oral health depends partly on interactions among microbes, saliva, diet, hygiene and the host.

THINK WITH DATA

Cavities / Dental Caries

How can caries develop?

Plaque bacteria can convert free sugars into acids. Repeated acid exposure can contribute to mineral loss from teeth.

Saliva, fluoride, plaque removal and healthier food environments can help shift the balance toward protection.

DMFT

D Decayed
M Missing due to caries
F Filled
T Teeth

DMFT is a population-level measure of dental caries experience in permanent teeth.

GHE DATA CHALLENGE

Two communities have the same average DMFT. Does that mean they have the same oral-health situation?

Not necessarily. Look at prevalence, untreated disease, distribution, age, income, geography and access to care.

Gum & Periodontal Health

From healthy gums to periodontal disease

Gingivitis involves inflammation of the gums. More severe periodontal disease can damage tissues and bone supporting the teeth.

Plaque control is important, but oral health is also influenced by broader factors including tobacco exposure, diabetes, access to care and social conditions.

REMEMBER

Oral health is connected to the rest of the body.

Understanding these connections requires evidence—not assumptions that one disease automatically causes another.

CONNECT

Oral Injuries, Orthodontics & Appearance

Oral Injuries

Falls, collisions and some recreational activities can cause oro-dental injuries. Safer environments and appropriate protective equipment can reduce risk.

Orthodontics

Orthodontic care can address alignment and bite problems and may also have psychosocial dimensions.

Why Does Beauty Matter?

Ideas about an attractive smile are shaped by culture, media and social expectations. Natural differences should never become reasons for shame or stigma.

GLOBAL AWARENESS

Oral Cancer Awareness

Oral cancer includes cancers affecting the lip, mouth and related areas. Major preventable risk factors include tobacco and alcohol, while areca-nut exposure is important in some populations.

HEALTH LITERACY PRINCIPLE

Persistent or unusual changes in the mouth should not simply be ignored. Appropriate assessment by a qualified health professional matters.

PREVENTION

Fluoride & Prevention

What fluoride does

Fluoride helps protect enamel, supports remineralization and increases resistance to acid-related mineral loss.

Where it comes from

Exposure may come from fluoridated toothpaste, drinking water and professionally applied fluoride products.

The equity lens

Population-level prevention can be important because people do not all have the same access to professional dental services.

Fluoride recommendations depend on age, exposure and local context. Follow current guidance from qualified oral-health professionals and public-health authorities.

CONNECT

Sugar & Oral Health

Free sugars are an important shared risk factor for dental caries and several other noncommunicable diseases.

Instead of simply asking, “Is sugar bad?”, global health asks how patterns of consumption, food environments, marketing, availability and affordability shape health.

THINK UPSTREAM

Individual choices happen inside environments.

Public health asks what makes healthier choices easier—or harder.

HEALTH EQUITY

SDH & Oral Health Equity

Oral health is shaped by much more than what happens in a bathroom.

Income

Affordability can influence food, prevention, coverage and dental care.

Education

Health literacy influences how people find, understand and use health information.

Place

Geography, transportation and local services influence access.

Policy

Public-health decisions can make prevention more or less accessible.

KNOWLEDGE CHECK

Oral Health — 12 MCQs

Think first. Open each question to check your answer and reasoning.

1. Which statement best describes oral health?
Answer: Oral health includes the health and functioning of the mouth, teeth and orofacial structures, as well as related wellbeing and participation.
2. What is one important role of saliva?
Answer: Saliva helps protect the mouth and contributes to maintaining the mineral balance of teeth.
3. What does the “F” in DMFT represent?
Answer: Filled teeth.
4. Why can the same average DMFT hide inequalities?
Answer: Two populations can have the same average while disease is distributed very differently among individuals or social groups.
5. How can free sugars contribute to dental caries?
Answer: Plaque bacteria can use free sugars to produce acids that contribute to mineral loss from teeth.
6. How does fluoride help prevent cavities?
Answer: Fluoride helps protect enamel, supports remineralization and makes tooth surfaces more resistant to acid-related damage.
7. Why can school-based oral-health programs be important?
Answer: They can reach many young people and may reduce barriers to prevention and health education.
8. Why is oral health an equity issue?
Answer: Income, education, geography, food environments, service availability and affordability can all influence oral-health opportunities and outcomes.
9. Which statement best fits the GHE approach to orthodontics and appearance?
Answer: Orthodontics can have functional and psychosocial dimensions, while ideas of beauty are influenced by culture and society and should not create stigma.
10. Why should oral-health statistics from different countries be compared carefully?
Answer: Definitions, age groups, years, surveillance methods and population coverage may differ.
11. Which sequence represents the GHE spatial learning pathway?
Answer: SELF → FAMILY & PEERS → COMMUNITY → COUNTRY → WORLD.
12. What should you ask when you see a surprising oral-health statistic?
Answer: Ask who was measured, where and when the data were collected, how the indicator was defined, and what the average might be hiding.
