GHE PRINCIPLE

Every difference matters. Explore it.

A global average can help us see the big picture, but averages can also hide important differences between people, places and circumstances.

WHY EXPLORE THE WORLD?

The world is not one average.

Countries differ. Communities differ. People live in different environments, have different opportunities and face different risks. Global health data help us recognize these patterns and ask better questions.

01

Countries differ

Health indicators can vary substantially between countries and regions.

02

Communities differ

National averages can hide differences within the same country.

03

Data reveal patterns

Indicators allow us to measure differences across places, populations and time.

04

Context matters

A number becomes meaningful when we understand its definition, population, place, time and source.

FROM DATA TO MAPS

Geography can make patterns visible.

Maps help us see where differences occur. But a map can show a pattern without automatically explaining why that pattern exists.

01

What does a map add?

A table gives us values. A map adds geography. Together, they can reveal patterns that may be difficult to see from individual numbers alone.

TABLE Gives values
MAP Shows geography
PATTERN Reveals differences
02

Before interpreting a map, ask.

  1. What? What indicator is being mapped?
  2. Who? Which population is represented?
  3. When? Which year or period?
  4. How? Is the measure observed, estimated or modelled?
  5. How certain? What uncertainty surrounds the estimate?

MAP LITERACY

Geography reveals differences. It does not automatically explain them.

A visible geographic pattern is a starting point, not a conclusion.

A map can help us see

  • Geographic distribution
  • Differences between places
  • Clusters and gradients
  • Regional patterns
  • Changes over time

We still need to explore

  • Definitions and methods
  • Population composition
  • Data quality and uncertainty
  • Social determinants of health
  • Environment and climate
  • Health systems
  • Historical and social context

SOURCE LITERACY

Not every data source answers the same question.

Official statistics, research estimates, analytical databases and educational visualizations can all be useful — but they are not interchangeable.

Official statistics

Produced by governments or international organizations using defined statistical systems.

Research estimates

Estimates generated through research methods, modelling or synthesis of multiple sources.

Analytical platforms

Systems that organize, visualize or analyze data from multiple sources.

Educational visualizations

Tools designed to help learners see and understand patterns in evidence.

Important: Some numbers are observed. Others are estimated. A useful explorer checks the method, source, definition and uncertainty before interpreting a result.
KNOWLEDGE CHECK

Test your global health explorer skills.

Try these questions before moving to the next topic.

Q1. What is the main purpose of a health indicator?
To measure a defined aspect of health or a factor related to health in a population.
Q2. Why can a global average hide important information?
Because people, communities and countries can have very different experiences even when their average values appear similar.
Q3. What does a map add to a table of data?
Geography. A map can make spatial patterns, differences and concentrations easier to see.
Q4. Does a geographic pattern automatically explain its cause?
No. A geographic pattern is evidence of a difference in distribution, not proof of why the difference exists.
Q5. Why should you check the year of an indicator?
Because conditions can change over time, and comparing values from different years may produce a misleading conclusion.
Q6. What does “population” tell you about an indicator?
It tells you who is included in the measurement, such as a particular age group, sex or population.
Q7. Why is the denominator important?
It defines the population or amount against which the numerator is compared and therefore affects the meaning of a rate or proportion.
Q8. What is one difference between an observed value and an estimate?
An observed value comes directly from measurement or reporting, while an estimate may be produced using statistical or modelling methods.
Q9. Name one social determinant of health.
Examples include education, economic conditions, housing, social conditions, working conditions and access to resources.
Q10. Why should health differences be investigated before being judged?
Because differences may have multiple explanations, and careful evidence is needed before deciding whether a difference is unfair, avoidable or remediable.
Q11. What is the GHE Data Rule?
“A number is not the answer. It is the beginning of a question.”
Q12. What should you do after discovering an interesting pattern?
Ask a better question. Check the definition, source, population, time, uncertainty and context, then explore what additional evidence could help explain the pattern.
