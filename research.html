What is research?

Research is a systematic way of asking questions, gathering and examining evidence, and developing knowledge that can help us understand problems and make better decisions.

In health sciences, research can help us measure health problems, understand their causes and determinants, develop or improve solutions, translate evidence into policy and practice, and evaluate whether an intervention or programme works.

01

Think like a researcher

Be curious. Be precise. Question assumptions. Look for evidence. Be transparent about what you know — and what you do not yet know.

THE FIRST STEP

Start with a good question

A strong research question gives your investigation direction. It helps you decide what evidence you need, who or what you need to study, and which methods may be appropriate.

FROM QUESTION TO KNOWLEDGE

Evidence does not speak for itself.

Finding evidence is only part of research. We also need to understand how evidence was produced, how strong it is, what its limitations are, and whether it can help answer our particular question.

That means learning to distinguish an interesting claim from a well-supported finding — and learning to communicate uncertainty responsibly.

QUESTION What do we want to know?
EVIDENCE What information can help us answer it?
INTERPRETATION What does the evidence actually tell us?
REFLECTION What are the limits, gaps and uncertainties?
ACTION What could responsible use of this knowledge change?
THE RESEARCH LANDSCAPE

One health question can lead to many kinds of research.

There is no single way to investigate a health problem. Different questions call for different research traditions — and sometimes several approaches can work together.

A POWERFUL GATEWAY

Implementation Research

Evidence can be convincing — yet a programme, policy or intervention may still work differently in the real world.

Implementation research asks how health interventions, programmes and policies are put into practice, what helps or hinders implementation, and how context influences what happens.

For young researchers, it offers a powerful way to connect evidence with action: not only asking “Does this work?” but also “How can this work here, for these people, in this setting?”

“Research can help us understand not only what should happen, but how change can happen responsibly in real-world settings.”

A NON-NEGOTIABLE PRINCIPLE

Good research is responsible research.

Health research can involve people, personal information, biological materials or sensitive questions. Young researchers should never independently collect or use human-participant data simply because a project is educational.

Ask first Discuss your idea with your teacher, school or appropriate supervisor.
Protect people Respect privacy, dignity, consent, safety and confidentiality.
Know the rules Human-participant research may require formal ethics review and other permissions.
Be honest Do not invent, manipulate or overstate findings.
