TOPIC 01 · DATA LITERACY

What exactly is health data?

Health data can come from many sources: surveys, clinical records, laboratories, sensors, registries, satellites, research studies, and many other systems.

But a number is not automatically evidence. Before interpreting data, ask where it came from, who was included, how it was measured, what is missing, and how certain we are.

Questions to explore
  • What makes data trustworthy?
  • Why does the denominator matter?
  • What is the difference between correlation and causation?
  • How can missing data change a conclusion?

TOPIC 02 · DATA VISUALIZATION

Can a picture change how we understand health?

Maps, charts, dashboards, and interactive visualizations can make complex health patterns easier to explore.

But visualization is not neutral. The choice of scale, colour, category, denominator, time period, and geographic boundary can change what a viewer notices.

Try asking
  • What does this graph show?
  • What does it hide?
  • Could another visualization tell a different story?
  • Is the comparison fair?

TOPIC 03 · GLOBAL HEALTH EVIDENCE

How do we measure the world's health?

Global health researchers combine enormous quantities of information to estimate patterns of disease, disability, injury, and premature death across populations.

Exploring these datasets can help students ask questions such as: Which conditions cause the greatest burden? How does the picture change by age, sex, location, or time?

Explore with evidence
  • Why might two countries have different health burdens?
  • What happens when a disease becomes less common but populations become older?
  • Why are estimates sometimes uncertain?
  • What can global datasets tell us—and what can they not tell us?

Topic 04 · Artificial Intelligence

What could AI change in global health?

Artificial intelligence can support tasks such as pattern recognition, prediction, information synthesis, clinical decision support, drug development, disease surveillance, and health system management.

But an AI system can also reproduce bias, make mistakes, be difficult to interpret, or perform differently in populations that were poorly represented in its development data.

Could AI help? What problem might it solve?
What evidence is needed? How would we know whether it works?
What could go wrong? Bias, errors, privacy, misuse?
Who should decide? Where should human oversight remain?

TOPIC 05 · GENOMIC & BIOMEDICAL DATA

When biology becomes data

Genomics connects biological science with computation and data science. Modern sequencing technologies can generate enormous datasets that require sophisticated methods to interpret.

For young Explorers, the gateway is not laboratory research. It is learning how biological information becomes data—and how researchers use evidence, computation, and responsible interpretation.

Questions to explore
  • What information is contained in a genome?
  • Why do researchers need very large datasets?
  • How can genomic data help understand disease?
  • Who should control access to genomic information?

Topic 07 · Health Equity

Who is visible in the data?

A dataset can look objective while still reflecting the inequalities of the world in which it was created.

If some communities are poorly represented, digital systems and AI models may work less well for them. Data innovation therefore needs an equity lens from the beginning.

Who is included? Which populations are represented?
Who is missing? Which groups may be invisible?
Who benefits? Who receives the value of innovation?
Who could be harmed? Could bias create unequal consequences?

Innovation Pathway

Turn a data question into a mini-proposal

You are not expected to build a clinical AI system or conduct advanced data science. Your challenge is to identify a meaningful problem, investigate the evidence, and design a clear, responsible idea.
