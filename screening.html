Screening

Screening helps identify certain health conditions or risks before symptoms become obvious. Understanding what screening can—and cannot—do is an important part of prevention, health literacy, and responsible decision-making.

WHY THIS MATTERS

Finding a health problem early can sometimes make a difference.

Screening is a public-health and clinical tool used for particular conditions and populations. It can help identify people who may benefit from further assessment, monitoring, or care.

01

Earlier Awareness

Some conditions can exist before noticeable symptoms appear. Appropriate screening may identify a possible concern earlier.

02

Informed Follow-Up

A screening result can help determine whether further assessment or discussion with a health professional may be appropriate.

03

Population Health

Screening programmes can also contribute to population health when they are supported by good evidence, appropriate follow-up, and equitable access.

PERSONAL HINTS

Be informed, not alarmed.

Screening is most useful when you understand its purpose, limitations, possible results, and what happens next.

01

Ask what the test is for.

Before accepting a health claim or test, understand what condition or risk it is designed to assess.

02

Know that screening has limits.

No screening test is perfect. A result may be reassuring without ruling out every possible health problem.

03

Do not diagnose yourself.

Screening is different from diagnosis. Concerning or unexpected results may need professional assessment.

04

Use trustworthy information.

Health decisions should be based on reliable evidence rather than fear, viral posts, advertisements, or unsupported claims.

EXPLORE

What exactly is screening?

Start by understanding the difference between screening, diagnosis, monitoring, and routine health care.

S

Screening

Testing or assessment offered to people who may not have symptoms, usually because evidence suggests that finding a condition earlier can provide a meaningful benefit.

D

Diagnosis

The process of determining whether a person has a particular condition, usually in response to symptoms, signs, risk factors, or an abnormal screening result.

F

Follow-Up

Further assessment may be needed after a screening result. Follow-up is an important part of a safe screening programme.

R

Risk Assessment

Personal and family history, age, environment, behaviours, and other factors can influence health risk and may affect appropriate care.

E

Evidence

Screening recommendations should be supported by evidence showing that benefits are likely to outweigh potential harms.

Q

Questions

Asking what is being tested, who benefits, how accurate the test is, and what happens afterward is part of health literacy.

LEARN

Screening is about probability, not certainty.

Understanding this idea is one of the most important lessons in health literacy.

01

A positive screen does not automatically mean disease.

A screening test may identify someone as more likely to have a condition. Additional assessment may then be needed to determine what is actually happening.

02

A negative screen does not guarantee perfect health.

Screening tests can miss conditions. A person should still pay attention to symptoms or concerns and seek appropriate professional advice.

03

Not every possible screening test is useful.

A screening programme needs evidence that the test is appropriate, that the condition matters, and that meaningful follow-up and treatment are available.

04

Age and risk matter.

Screening recommendations can differ according to age, sex, personal history, family history, risk factors, and the health system in which someone receives care.

05

Screening only works as part of a system.

A test alone is not enough. Effective programmes require appropriate communication, access, follow-up, diagnosis, treatment, monitoring, and evaluation.

SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH

Access to screening is not equal everywhere.

Whether someone can benefit from screening depends on more than the existence of a test. Health systems, geography, cost, information, trust, and follow-up all matter.

Access

A screening programme cannot improve health equitably if people cannot reach or afford appropriate services.

Health Literacy

People need understandable information to make informed decisions about screening.

Geography

Rural, remote, northern, and underserved communities may face different barriers to preventive services.

Trust

Trust in health professionals, institutions, information, and communities can influence whether people participate in health services.

Follow-Up

Screening has limited value when people cannot access timely diagnostic assessment and appropriate care afterward.

Equity

Public-health programmes should consider who benefits, who is missed, and which barriers create avoidable differences in health.

GLOBAL LEADERSHIP & EVIDENCE

Ask better questions about screening.

Health leadership requires more than knowing that a test exists. It requires understanding evidence, benefits, limitations, implementation, and equity.

ASK

What is being screened?

Identify the condition, risk, or health problem that the screening programme is intended to detect.

CHECK

What evidence supports it?

Look for recommendations and evidence from credible health authorities and scientific sources.

BALANCE

What are the benefits and limitations?

Consider accuracy, false results, follow-up, potential harms, resources, and the likelihood of meaningful benefit.

EQUITY

Who might be left out?

A strong programme considers whether people in different communities can access and benefit from the service.

ONE STEP

Before believing a screening claim, ask: “What is the evidence?”

Choose one screening claim you encounter in a conversation, advertisement, or online post. Identify what it says, who recommends it, and what evidence supports the recommendation.
