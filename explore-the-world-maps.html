From Data to Maps

Geography can make patterns visible. Maps help us see where differences occur.

But a map can show us a pattern without automatically explaining it.

Look first. Interpret carefully.
Visual discovery

What does a map add?

A table tells us values. A map can help us see geographic patterns, concentrations, gradients and differences between places.

Higher Intermediate Lower

A map is a way of asking a question.

Where are the values higher?

Where are they lower?

Are there clusters or gradients?

Do neighboring places look similar?

What happens when we change the geographic scale?

Become a map reader

Before interpreting a map, ask five questions.

01

What?

What indicator is being mapped?

02

Who?

Which population does the indicator describe?

03

When?

Which year or period does the map represent?

04

How?

Was the value observed, estimated or modelled?

05

How certain?

Is uncertainty reported?

Think like a global health explorer

Geography reveals differences. It does not automatically explain them.

What we can observe

  • Geographic distribution
  • Differences between places
  • Concentrations and clusters
  • Changes over time
  • Differences between regions

What we still need to explore

  • Definitions and methods
  • Population composition
  • Data quality and uncertainty
  • Social determinants
  • Environmental conditions
  • Health systems and services
  • Historical and social context
A map can reveal a pattern. It does not by itself tell us why that pattern exists.
