Where do children affected by malnutrition live?

Global health data can tell us not only how many children are affected by malnutrition, but also where those children live. In this exploration, we look at the geographic distribution of stunting, wasting and overweight among children under 5 in 2024.

EXPLORE THE DATA

Three conditions. Three different patterns.

Stunting, wasting and overweight are different forms of malnutrition. Their global distributions are not identical. Looking carefully at the percentages helps us ask better questions about population size, nutrition, living conditions, food systems and health systems.

DATA LITERACY

A share is not the same as a prevalence

This is one of the most important ideas in reading global health data correctly.

WHAT THE CHART SHOWS

Share of the global burden

“Asia 51%” means that 51% of all children under 5 affected by stunting worldwide lived in Asia in 2024.

WHAT IT DOES NOT SHOW

Prevalence within Asia

It does not mean that 51% of children in Asia were stunted. That would require a different denominator.

🔎

Always ask: “51% of what?”

THINK BEYOND ONE CONDITION

Can undernutrition and overweight exist in the same region?

Yes. A population can experience different forms of malnutrition at the same time. This is sometimes described as the double burden of malnutrition.

The Asia figures provide an opportunity to explore this idea: a large share of the global burden of both stunting and childhood overweight is found in the same broad region.

GHE DATA SKILL

Read the denominator before you interpret the percentage.

A percentage only has meaning when we know what the percentage is a percentage of. The denominator tells us what question the number can answer.

REFLECT

One number can tell a story — if you know what it means.

Global health is full of percentages. Your job as a data-literate explorer is to pause, identify the denominator, understand the population being described, and then ask what the data can — and cannot — tell you.

Explore the number. Understand the denominator. Ask a better question.
